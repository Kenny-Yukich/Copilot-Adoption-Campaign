# Training Program

Three phases. Each is 30 minutes. Anything longer and attendance collapses; anything shorter and nothing sticks.

## Design principles

- **Teach tasks, not features.** Never demo a ribbon button. Demo a job someone in the room does.
- **The first attempt must succeed.** Have participants run one prompt live, on their own real work, before they leave. A person whose first attempt fails will not make a second attempt for weeks.
- **Deliver by department, not by mixed audience.** Same slides, different examples. A 12-person department session outperforms a 60-person all-hands by a wide margin.
- **No recordings as a substitute.** Record for people who missed it, but recording-only rollouts produce near-zero adoption.

## Phase 1 — "It works on your stuff" (week 2)

Goal: every attendee gets one successful result on their own document.

1. What the tool can see (this is the trust conversation — 5 min, and it must come first)
2. The four-part prompt: **role, input, output format, constraint**
3. Live exercise: summarize a document you already have open
4. Live exercise: draft a reply to an email in your own inbox
5. Where to find the prompt gallery for your department

Ends with: everyone has produced one usable output.

## Phase 2 — "Make it yours" (week 5)

Goal: move from one-off use to a repeated habit.

1. Iterating instead of restarting — how to correct output rather than re-prompting from scratch
2. Working across files and meetings (referencing documents, meeting recaps)
3. Building a personal 3-prompt shortlist for their actual weekly routine
4. When *not* to use it: judgment calls, anything requiring verification, anything where you can't check the answer
5. How to tell you about a win (this feeds the champion spotlight pipeline)

Ends with: each person has written down three prompts they will use this week.

## Phase 3 — "Beyond the chat box" (week 9)

Goal: convert repeated prompts into something durable, and find the automation candidates.

1. Review what stuck and what didn't — honestly, out loud
2. Prompts that should become templates or agents
3. Intro to what an agent is, in plain terms, with one demo relevant to that department
4. Submitting an automation request (give them a real intake path)

Ends with: at least one automation candidate per department in your backlog.

## Facilitation notes

- Skepticism in a manufacturing or trades environment is usually about **job security or data trust**, not the technology. Address both in the first five minutes or spend the rest of the session talking past the room.
- The single most effective demo is one you didn't prepare: ask someone to hand you a real task, live. It fails occasionally. That's fine — how you recover from the failure is itself the most credible thing you'll do all session.
- Never oversell. "This is bad at arithmetic and it will confidently make things up; here's how you check it" buys you more adoption than any capability claim.
